# Astronaut Health Monitoring Challenge

## Challenge

Build visual health-monitoring software that gathers health indicators, helps astronauts assess their physical and mental health, and provides guidance on preventive actions, treatments, and health improvements during long-duration missions.

## Why It Matters

Long missions increase exposure to spaceflight hazards while communication delays and limited supplies reduce access to Earth-based medical support. Astronauts need to recognize health changes and make informed assessments themselves.

NASA's Human Research Program identifies five interacting hazards:

- **Space radiation:** Can damage DNA and increase cataract and cancer risks.
- **Isolation and confinement:** Can affect mood and behavioral health.
- **Distance from Earth:** Delays communication and limits emergency assistance and resupply.
- **Altered gravity:** Affects bones, muscles, balance, circulation, and physical performance.
- **Hostile, closed environments:** Create additional risks to crew health and well-being.

## Health Areas to Monitor

- **Blood and immunity:** Changes in blood-cell production, anemia, and vulnerability to infection.
- **Heart and metabolism:** Vascular changes, cardiovascular risks, insulin resistance, and blood sugar changes.
- **Bones and muscles:** Loss of bone density, muscle mass, and strength.
- **Brain and nervous system:** Disorientation, motion sickness, balance problems, and slower reflexes.
- **Daily well-being:** Sleep, mood, nutrition, and behavioral health.
- **Radiation exposure:** Accumulated exposure and associated health risks.

## Core Objectives

1. **Collect health data:** Support inputs such as typed measurements, selections, camera images, or medical images. Example indicators include blood pressure, heart rate, blood glucose, body temperature, sleep duration, and mood.
2. **Make health status understandable:** Present measurements, trends, and assessment information in a clear visual interface.
3. **Support informed action:** Explain measurements and risks, identify results that warrant crew or flight-surgeon attention, and provide guidance on prevention and health improvement.
4. **Ground the solution in research:** Use NASA Human Research Program and other public space-agency resources to select relevant indicators and understand spaceflight-related changes.

## Design Questions

- Which measurements are feasible in space, and why is each needed?
- How do spaceflight hazards affect each indicator?
- When should a result prompt attention from the crew or ground medical teams?
- What actions could prevent a condition from becoming critical?
- How can the interface help astronauts with limited medical knowledge?

## Optional Features

- Interactive body diagram linking body regions to health information.
- Dynamic charts showing changes over time.
- Color-coded status indicators with simple explanations.
- Routine reminders for measurements and health-supporting activities.
- Gamification to encourage participation.
- Support for spacecraft, space stations, rovers, and lunar or planetary bases.

## Supporting Research Context

The Canadian Space Agency's **CANS** instrument is being developed to measure neutron radiation continuously and autonomously. It builds on earlier ISS radiation-detection experiments and illustrates the value of ongoing exposure monitoring for astronaut protection.

ISS research helps identify health risks and evaluate countermeasures for longer Moon and Mars missions. Space-related changes also resemble aspects of aging and sedentary lifestyles, informing research on osteoporosis, balance disorders, cardiovascular disease, and Type 2 diabetes on Earth.
